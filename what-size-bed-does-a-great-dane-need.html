What Size Bed Does a Great Dane Need? Measurements by Age, Sleeping Style and Brand Size Charts

Measure your dog, pick a sleeping style, and get a minimum bed length you can compare against any brand's size chart.

A Great Dane bed has to fit a dog who stands around 30 to 32 inches at the shoulder, so the label on the box matters less than the actual inside dimensions. This guide gives you a measuring method, a calculator that turns your numbers into a minimum bed length, and the size-chart data that is published for beds in this size class.

Quick answer: what size bed does a Great Dane need?

Plan on a sleeping surface of at least 50 × 36 inches for an adult Dane. That is the size one bed maker lists for the breed, and its chart tops out at dogs up to 46 inches long. Measure your own dog from nose to base of tail, add at least 4 inches, and add a quarter more length if your dog sprawls. Standard “XL” beds are usually too short.

Why Great Dane bed sizing goes wrong

Most bed lines are labelled Small to XL and built around the average pet. A Dane sits beyond that scale. Kuranda's size chart, for example, puts the Boxer and Labrador in its 40 × 25 inch Large and the German Shepherd in its 44 × 27 inch X-Large, then lists the Great Dane under the 50 × 36 inch XX-Large, according to the manufacturer's chart. The step from “big dog” to “Dane” is a full size band, not a small adjustment.

Two other mistakes are common. The first is sizing by weight, because a lean 120-pound Dane and a heavy 160-pound Dane may be the same length. The second is sizing for a puppy. A bed that fits at four months will be a bolster-sized pillow within a year, so decide up front whether you are buying once for adult size or planning a cheap interim bed.

How to measure a Great Dane for a bed

You need a soft tape, a calm dog and ideally a helper with treats. Chewy's sizing guide advises measuring when the dog is calm or tired, taking length from the tip of the nose to the tail, and checking width across the widest part of the body, usually shoulder to shoulder, as described in its dog bed size article.

Length: nose to base of tail

Stop at the base of the tail, not the tip. Kuranda's instruction is to measure “from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail and add 4 inches,” and to choose a bed whose length is at least that number rather than sizing down. When your dog falls between two sizes, Chewy's rule is to take the bigger bed.

Width and height

Measure shoulder to shoulder while the dog stands, then compare it with the bed's inside width, not the outer frame. PetMD quotes veterinarian Dr. Robin Downing advising that a giant-breed bed should be about one-fourth longer than the dog lying down and about the same proportion wider than the dog is tall, in its guide to beds for giant breeds. The same page recommends a slightly raised bed, around one to two feet off the floor, as better suited to giant dogs than a bed they have to climb into.

Bed size by age: what a growing Dane needs

Danes keep growing for a long time. Canine Journal's growth chart says they typically reach full height around eighteen months and keep gaining weight until about 24 months, in its Great Dane growth guide. Its adult figures are roughly 32 inches at the shoulder for males and 30 inches for females. Re-measure at the stages above, and use the calculator again whenever your dog gains a visible amount of length.

Sleeping style changes the size you need

Chewy's guide sorts dogs into three groups: sprawlers who spread out in all directions, curlers who sleep in a tight ball, and leaners who prefer to push against something soft. For each group the bed advice differs, and the calculator uses it.

  • Curlers: take the +4 inch rule and you are covered. A bed with raised edges is a bonus.
  • Side sleepers who stretch out: use the quarter-longer rule, because the dog's length lying flat is the number that matters.
  • Sprawlers: pick the bigger option when in doubt. Floor space around the bed matters too, since a Dane who overflows will take over the rug.
  • Leaners: Chewy's chart points to bolster beds with lots of support. Remember that bolsters shrink the usable surface, so measure the inside dimension.

Brand size charts compared

Brand names are unreliable because “Large” means different things. The only dependable method is to compare the stated inside dimensions with your own measurement. The Kuranda chart is a useful reference because it publishes both bed sizes and the longest dog each one suits.

Source: Kuranda's published size chart. A dog who measures 47 inches or more nose to base of tail already exceeds the largest size in that chart, which is why some owners of big males look at custom beds or place two beds together.

Also check the weight rating, not just the length. Kuranda's Great Dane page lists a weight capacity of up to 100 lb for its standard bed and up to 250 lb for its aluminum bed, which shows how quickly a standard rating can be too low for a 140-pound dog. Whichever brand you shop, look for a stated rating well above your dog's weight.

Orthopedic foam, height and surface

For giant breeds, thickness matters as much as footprint. PetMD cites Dr. Hyunmin Kim as saying a minimum of 2 inches of memory foam is needed for a bed to be labelled orthopedic, and that most orthopedic memory foam beds carry at least 4 inches, some up to 7. A thin 2-inch layer under a 140-pound dog bottoms out quickly, so favor the thicker end.

PetMD also notes that a nonslip surface and a mat or rug under the bed help a dog get on and off. A Dane's long legs can slide on a hard floor when stepping onto a smooth bed cover, so grip is worth checking in person if you can.

Worked examples

Example 1: the curler

A 42-inch male who curls up needs 42 + 4 = 46 inches of length by the Kuranda rule. The quarter-longer rule gives 52.5, but it is aimed at dogs lying stretched out. A 50 × 36 bed covers this dog with room to spare.

Example 2: the sprawler

A 45-inch dog who sprawls would need 49 inches by the +4 rule and about 56 inches by the quarter-longer rule. That is already past the 50-inch bed. Look for a larger single bed, or set a second mat beside it so legs have somewhere to go.

Example 3: the growing puppy

A 4-month-old male at about 55 pounds, by the growth chart above, is still far from adult size. Measure him; a mid-size bed may fit now, but he will outgrow it quickly. Buy a cheap, washable interim bed or go straight to the adult size and add a folded blanket to shrink the space for the first months.

A short buying checklist

  1. Measure your dog lying in the usual sleeping position, nose to base of tail.
  2. Run the calculator and note the target length.
  3. Compare against the inside dimensions listed by the maker, not the size name.
  4. Check the foam thickness and the weight rating.
  5. Confirm the floor space: a 50 × 36 inch bed needs a clear area noticeably larger than that.
  6. Keep the receipt or confirm a return policy, because fit is hard to judge from a product page.

FAQ: Great Dane bed sizes

What size bed does an adult Great Dane need?

At least 50 × 36 inches is the size one bed maker lists for the breed. Measure your own dog, add 4 inches to the nose-to-tail length, and choose a longer bed if your dog sprawls.

Is an XL dog bed big enough for a Great Dane?

Often not. In the Kuranda chart the X-Large is 44 × 27 inches and lists the German Shepherd, while the Great Dane sits in the 50 × 36 inch XX-Large.

Should I size a Great Dane bed by weight?

Length and sleeping style are better guides. Use weight only to check that the bed's weight rating is well above your dog.

When do I stop needing bigger beds as the puppy grows?

Canine Journal says Danes typically reach full height around 18 months and keep gaining weight until about 24 months, so re-measure through the second year.

How thick should an orthopedic bed be?

PetMD quotes a veterinary source who says 2 inches of memory foam is the minimum to be labelled orthopedic and most such beds have at least 4 inches. A giant breed does better at the thicker end.

Should the bed be raised off the floor?

PetMD says a bed about one to two feet above the ground often suits giant breeds, as long as the dog does not have to jump or climb to get on it.

The short version

For the question of what size bed a Great Dane needs, start with a tape measure rather than a label. Take the nose-to-base-of-tail length, add at least 4 inches, add a quarter more if your dog stretches out, and compare the result with the inside dimensions of the bed you are considering. Most adult Danes land at 50 inches of length or more.
