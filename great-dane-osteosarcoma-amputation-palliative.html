Great Dane Osteosarcoma in a 150 lb Dog: Amputation or Palliative Care?

Survival numbers, real owner costs and mobility data in one place, so you can make the call in a single evening instead of a week of searching.

Home›Blog›Great Dane Osteosarcoma: Amputation vs Palliative Care

Published: 2026-10-12 · Updated: 2026-10-12 · Great Dane Hub

A Great Dane osteosarcoma diagnosis usually arrives with a limp, an X-ray and a decision you have days, not months, to make. Most owners of a 150 lb dog end up choosing between amputation (with or without chemotherapy) and palliative care built around pain control and radiation. This guide puts the numbers side by side so you can get to a decision fast.

Quick answer

  • Amputation alone: median survival of about 4 to 5 months. Amputation is also the most direct way to remove the pain source.
  • Amputation plus chemotherapy: median survival of 10 to 12 months, and about half of dogs reach one year.
  • Palliative radiation plus pain medication: about 109 to 119 days (roughly 3.5 to 4 months) in the largest recent case series.
  • Body weight matters for recovery, not for radiation survival. Heavier, fatter dogs scored lower on quality of life after amputation. Weight did not change survival after palliative radiation.
  • Spread already present at diagnosis? Median survival drops to about 2 months whichever route you choose.

Why Great Danes get bone cancer so often

Appendicular osteosarcoma, which is bone cancer of the legs, mostly affects large and giant dogs. Great Danes are named among the overrepresented breeds alongside Greyhounds and Rottweilers in a Colorado State University radiation study of 165 dogs. In that group, 77% of tumors were in a front leg. The radius (39%) and humerus (36%) were the most common sites. Front legs carry most of a Dane's weight, so this detail matters when you think about life on three legs.

Veterinary oncologists have a blunt rule for giant breeds: lameness plus swelling near the end of a long bone "is osteosarcoma until proven otherwise." If your Dane's limp keeps coming back, don't keep resting it for weeks. Get X-rays now. A short delay costs you treatment options. A false alarm costs one vet visit.

Bone cancer is not the only threat on your list. Danes face a lifetime risk of stomach torsion that is covered in our guide to Great Dane bloat risk statistics. It's worth knowing because a bloat episode in a dog already on pain medication is a second emergency you don't want to face unprepared.

How long does a Great Dane live with osteosarcoma after each option?

Expect about 4 to 5 months with amputation alone and 10 to 12 months with amputation plus chemotherapy. Palliative radiation gives roughly 3.5 to 4 months. These are medians: half of dogs live longer and half live shorter.

The amputation figures come from a veterinary oncology summary published by dvm360. With amputation alone, 10% of dogs reach one year and 2% reach two years. Adding chemotherapy raises one-year survival to 50%, with 15% to 20% reaching two years. The same source explains why: although only 10% to 15% of dogs have visible spread at diagnosis, 90% die of metastasis within a year without systemic treatment. The cancer has usually seeded the lungs microscopically before anyone sees it.

What shortens survival

The negative prognostic factors listed by dvm360 are metastatic disease, young age, large tumor size, a tumor in the proximal humerus (upper front leg near the shoulder), high serum alkaline phosphatase and high histologic grade. Ask your vet which of these apply to your dog. It takes two minutes and makes the survival table above personal instead of generic.

Keep the bigger picture in mind too. The breed's normal lifespan is already short, as our Great Dane lifespan by weight article explains. For a 9-year-old Dane, a 10 to 12 month median is a large share of the time it had left anyway.

Can a 150 lb Great Dane walk well on three legs?

Most dogs adapt well after amputation, but weight works against you. In a study of 64 dogs, higher body weight and higher body condition scores were linked to lower quality-of-life scores after surgery.

That study, Dickerson and colleagues in JAVMA (2015), still has encouraging headline numbers. 88% of owners reported a complete or nearly complete return to the dog's previous quality of life. 78% said recovery was better than expected, and 91% saw no change in attitude. Overall, 86% would make the same decision again, 6% would not and 8% were unsure. The authors' practical takeaway was to keep amputated dogs lean after surgery.

What this means for a giant dog

  • Lean beats heavy. A Dane at 150 lb with visible waist and palpable ribs is a better candidate than one at 150 lb carrying extra fat. Fat can be lost. Bone cannot be added.
  • The other legs matter. Ask for an orthopedic check of the three remaining legs, especially the opposite front leg if a front limb is coming off. Arthritis or a cruciate problem there changes the picture.
  • Floors and stairs. A tripod giant slipping on tile is a real problem. Rugs, ramps and a harness with a handle are cheap and fast to set up.

Front leg vs hind leg

Most Dane tumors are in the front legs, and the front end carries more of the body weight. That doesn't rule amputation out, since most study dogs adapted. It is the reason your surgeon will look hard at the remaining front leg and your dog's overall condition before recommending surgery.

What does palliative care actually include?

Palliative care means keeping the leg and treating the pain. It usually combines oral pain medication, palliative radiation and sometimes bisphosphonate drugs, which slow bone breakdown.

Pain medication

The options named by the dvm360 oncology summary are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, tramadol, gabapentin and possibly bisphosphonates. Bone cancer pain tends to escalate, so plan for dose changes rather than a single prescription.

Palliative radiation

The Colorado State series treated 165 dogs with two 8 Gy doses on consecutive days. Median survival from the first radiation dose was 119 days with bisphosphonates and 109 days without, a difference that was not statistically significant. Two findings matter most for Dane owners:

  • Weight and giant-breed status did not change survival. Dogs under 39 kg (86 lb) did no differently from heavier dogs, and the heaviest dog weighed 93.6 kg (about 206 lb). Nine of the dogs were Great Danes.
  • The bone can still break. Twelve dogs (7%) later had the leg amputated anyway. Where records were available, half of those amputations were for a confirmed pathologic fracture, meaning the bone weakened by the tumor broke. A fracture in a 150 lb dog is an emergency, so ask in advance what your plan is if it happens.

Age also mattered in that study. Dogs aged 9 or younger had a median of 125 days, compared with 96 days for older dogs. For dogs that already had confirmed spread, the medians were 83.5 days with bisphosphonates and 57 days without.

How much does each path cost?

There is no national price list. For a dog around 150 lb, owners on the Tripawds forum have reported amputation bills of roughly $4,600 plus over $2,300 for diagnostics, and chemotherapy estimates around $4,000.

Those figures come from a Tripawds owner cost thread. A poster from Newark, Delaware, listed a 155 lb dog's amputation in October 2017 at $4,613 plus $2,325 for diagnostics, with five carboplatin sessions estimated at $4,000. On the same page, amputations for smaller dogs ranged from $1,005 for an outpatient case in Virginia to about $6,800 at a California hospital, and one owner paid $300 per carboplatin session. These are 2017–2019 prices, so expect higher numbers today.

Why giant dogs cost more

  • Drug doses scale with size. Anesthesia, pain medication and chemotherapy are dosed by weight or body surface area, so a Dane uses far more than a Labrador.
  • Hospital handling. Moving and nursing a 150 lb dog after surgery can take extra staff.
  • Palliative care is not "free." Repeated pain-drug refills, radiation sessions and possible emergency fracture care add up over months. Ask for a written estimate for each path, then enter both into the planner below.

The 7-day decision checklist

You can do the whole decision in a week with these steps, in this order:

  1. Day 1: Chest X-rays (3 views) and a lymph node check. Screening for spread is mandatory before amputation. It changes the survival table more than anything else.
  2. Day 1: Start pain control today. Don't wait for the decision. Ask which drug combination fits your dog's kidneys and stomach.
  3. Day 2: Full orthopedic and neurologic exam. Focus on the three legs that will remain.
  4. Day 2: Honest body condition score. Ask your vet to score your dog and say whether weight loss before or after surgery is realistic.
  5. Day 3: Get two written estimates. One for amputation (with and without chemo) and one for palliative radiation plus medication.
  6. Day 4: Set up the house. Non-slip runners, a ramp for the car and a lifting harness. Do it now so you're ready for either path.
  7. Days 5–7: Decide and book. Run the planner above with your real numbers. Then pick the path that matches your dog's condition, your budget and the time you can give to aftercare.

Common mistakes owners make

  • Treating a recurring limp as a sprain for weeks. In a giant breed, a long-bone limp with swelling needs X-rays, not just rest.
  • Assuming a big dog can't manage three legs. Weight lowers the odds of an excellent recovery but does not rule it out. Most owners in the JAVMA study would choose amputation again.
  • Skipping chest X-rays to save money. If the cancer has already spread, median survival is about 2 months. Paying for major surgery without knowing that is the costliest mistake on this list.
  • Picking palliative care without a fracture plan. Half of the later amputations in the radiation study with known reasons were for a broken bone. Decide in advance whether you would amputate or euthanize if that happens.
  • Letting weight creep up after surgery. Less exercise plus the same food equals a heavier tripod. Cut portions as soon as activity drops.
  • Comparing sticker prices only. Compare the cost per expected month of good-quality life. The planner calculates it for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is amputation worth it for a 150 lb Great Dane with osteosarcoma?

Often yes, if X-rays show no spread and the remaining legs are healthy. Amputation removes the painful bone, and adding chemotherapy brings median survival to 10–12 months. Weight and excess body fat lower the odds of an excellent recovery, so a lean dog is the better candidate.

How long can a Great Dane live with osteosarcoma without amputation?

With palliative radiation and pain medication, median survival in a 165-dog study was about 109–119 days. If the cancer had already spread, the medians were 57–83.5 days.

Does chemotherapy make dogs as sick as people?

Usually not. Veterinary oncologists report that most dogs tolerate chemotherapy well. Carboplatin, a common choice, can start once the surgical incision has healed.

How quickly do dogs adapt to three legs?

Many owners in the JAVMA study said recovery was faster and better than expected, and 88% reported a full or nearly full return to previous quality of life. Heavier dogs tend to need more time and rehab support.

What is the biggest risk of choosing palliative care?

Pain that outgrows the medication, and a pathologic fracture of the tumor-weakened bone. In the Colorado State radiation study, 7% of dogs later had amputation, and half of those with known reasons had a confirmed fracture.

How much does osteosarcoma treatment cost for a giant dog?

One owner of a 155 lb dog reported $4,613 for amputation plus $2,325 for diagnostics in 2017, with chemotherapy estimated at $4,000. Prices vary widely by region and have risen since, so get written estimates.

The short version

For a 150 lb Great Dane with osteosarcoma and clear chest X-rays, amputation plus chemotherapy gives the longest median survival, at 10 to 12 months. A lean body condition and healthy remaining legs are what make it work. Palliative radiation keeps the leg and buys about 3.5 to 4 months, whatever the dog weighs, but it carries a real fracture risk. Check for spread first, get two written quotes, run the planner, and you'll have a decision you can stand behind within a week.
