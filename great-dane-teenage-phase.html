The Great Dane Teenage Phase: Nipping, Zoomies and Testing Limits From 6 to 24 Months, and What Actually Works

A practical plan for playful mouthing, evening zoomies, and familiar cues that stop working around distractions.

Suddenly living with the Great Dane teenage phase can feel like sharing your home with a puppy who has gained reach, speed, and selective hearing overnight. This guide covers nipping, zoomies, and everyday boundary training across the 6-to-24-month planning window, with a practical way to decide what to change before the next difficult evening.

Quick answer: what actually works?

Prevent the situations your Dane cannot manage yet, reward a specific replacement behavior, and practice it somewhere easier before adding excitement. For playful mouthing, prepare a toy and an exit behind a gate; for zoomies, clear space; for ignored cues, lower the difficulty. Age alone is not a training plan.

When does the Great Dane teenage phase start and end?

Use 6 to 24 months as a flexible planning window, not a breed-specific countdown. Woodgreen's behavior and training specialist Lindsay Arliss explains that canine adolescence can begin around five months and continue until two years, with considerable individual variation.

Her adolescent-dog guidance recommends revisiting basic training and using management when previous skills become unreliable. That supports a useful expectation: your Dane may need easier practice again even when the behavior seemed learned.

Why does a teenage Dane suddenly ignore familiar cues?

A temporary dip in responsiveness is supported by canine research, but it does not explain every missed cue. Check the setting, the reward, and whether the dog can perform the task there before deciding your dog is deliberately challenging you.

A Newcastle University report on Lucy Asher's study describes obedience testing in 69 Labradors, Golden Retrievers, and their crosses at five and eight months. Dogs were less responsive to their caregiver's sit cue at eight months; the same pattern did not appear with an unfamiliar person. These were not Great Danes, so the study cannot establish a Dane-specific worst month.

Change the test before repeating the cue

Suppose your Dane sits promptly in the kitchen but watches another dog instead on the sidewalk. Try the same cue farther from the distraction, with your dog already looking toward you. Reward the response and move away together. If that remains too difficult, stop testing the sit and choose a quieter route.

Write down the context precisely: “looked at me in the driveway” is more useful than “obedient today.” It tells you where to start the next practice. Avoid stacking several requests together when you are still finding a successful starting point.

How do you stop playful nipping without starting a wrestling match?

Prepare an appropriate object for the mouth, stop adding exciting movement, and create space if the interaction becomes difficult. Work on what your dog should do before the familiar grabbing moment arrives.

Dogs Trust's behavior team links mouthing with excitement and frustration, recommends redirecting to suitable toys, and describes briefly separating with a gate when needed. It cautions that shouting or pushing can worsen the interaction and that rapidly pulling hands or clothing away can turn it into a game.

Rehearse the leash-pickup moment

Here is a sample setup to adapt. Put a familiar toy near the door while your Dane is elsewhere. Return with your dog, reach toward the leash, and reward quiet standing before touching it. End there if that is the easiest successful version. Later, try lifting the leash slightly and rewarding the same quiet stance.

If grabbing starts, prioritize getting space rather than completing the exercise. Do not pry the mouth open or turn the event into tug with your sleeve. Reset the environment and make the next rehearsal smaller. If you repeatedly need an emergency exit, arrange individual help rather than repeating the scenario.

Keep play and handling separate

Choose a clear invitation to begin toy play and a predictable finish. Avoid using your hands as moving targets. For an illustrative end routine, finish the game, put the toy aside when your dog releases it voluntarily, and offer a quiet activity in a familiar resting area. This is a suggested routine, not a promise that every dog will settle immediately.

Are zoomies a sign your Dane needs more exercise?

Not necessarily. Zoomies are bursts of running, and their context matters more than a single interpretation such as “under-exercised.” Start with the environment and the pattern you observe.

Cornell's explanation of zoomies calls them frenetic random activity periods, or FRAPs, and notes that no single specific cause is known. They may occur with excitement or after under-stimulation. Cornell recommends keeping zooming dogs away from stairs, slippery floors, obstacles, and roads.

Make an evening map

Sketch where the running usually begins and where it goes. Mark the narrow doorway, the coffee table, the stairs, and the place another pet rests. Rearrange that route before the next evening. Move people out of the running path instead of trying to catch a fast-moving Dane by the collar.

Then record what came beforehand: a visitor, toy play, a long quiet afternoon, or an unusually busy outing. These are observations, not explanations. Compare similar evenings before changing the routine. Adding more fetch after every burst makes it harder to learn whether the preceding activity was already too exciting.

Give the day a quiet ending

For a trial routine, follow an ordinary outing with a familiar sniffing opportunity and a quiet period at home. Keep the surroundings boring enough that you can notice what your dog chooses to do. You are testing a household routine, not prescribing a universal exercise quota for giant-breed dogs.

How do you set limits your teenage Dane can understand?

Describe the behavior you will reward in a particular situation, then arrange the surroundings so your dog can practice it. “Stand on the floor when a visitor enters” gives you something observable to work toward.

Write rules as actions

Instead of “no being wild at the door,” write “wait behind the gate while the visitor comes inside.” Instead of “stop bothering us at dinner,” write “rest on the mat beside the table.” Decide what the adults will do before you start; the dog cannot follow a household rule that changes with each person.

For a sample visitor rehearsal, ask a familiar adult to enter while your Dane remains comfortably separated. Reward quiet observation. Have the visitor sit rather than reach toward the dog. End the practice there if that is enough work for today. A greeting is optional; completing the quiet setup is a worthwhile endpoint.

Train resting as its own task

Choose a mat with room for your Dane to stretch out away from the walkway. Begin when the room is already quiet. Watch for voluntary resting and use calm reward delivery, as illustrated in the video above. If approaching with food makes your dog pop up, pause and reconsider the setup instead of repeating down cues.

This skill has a practical purpose beyond the living room. If travel is on your calendar, our guide to flying with a Great Dane explains the situations you may need to prepare for; practice the quiet household version before expecting a settle in an unfamiliar terminal.

A step-by-step reset for the next difficult evening

Choose one recurring event and change its setup. The sequence below is an editorial practice plan, not a validated behavior program or a guaranteed timetable.

  1. Name the event. Write “grabs my cuff when I collect the leash,” rather than “tests every boundary.” Include what you were doing immediately beforehand.
  2. Prepare the space. Put the toy within adult reach, clear the doorway, and arrange a comfortable barrier before you bring your dog into the situation.
  3. Choose a replacement. Quiet standing, looking toward you, or resting on a mat are clearer targets than “be good.” Choose only the one needed for this event.
  4. Rehearse an easier version. Reach toward the leash without picking it up, or ask the visitor to remain seated. Reward the response you wanted and finish while the setup is manageable.
  5. Change one feature. Add the leash movement or the visitor standing up on a later attempt. If the response disappears, return to the easier version.
  6. Log the result. Record the setting, whether the replacement happened, and whether you needed to separate. Use those observations to plan tomorrow's attempt.

Keep a useful record

A sample entry could read: “Evening, quiet hallway, leash on hook. Reached toward it; dog stayed on floor. Picking it up led to grabbing, so next practice ends before that movement.” This gives another household member an actionable starting point. It also documents progress that an all-or-nothing label would hide.

Look for a practical trend: easier redirection, fewer interrupted rehearsals, or the same skill working in a slightly harder setting. Avoid announcing success from a single quiet evening or treating one difficult day as proof that training has failed.

Common mistakes during the Great Dane teenage phase

The most useful correction is usually to make the next practice clearer. These examples help you inspect your own routine without assigning motives to your dog.

  • Repeating a cue through rising excitement. Pause, change the setting, and give the next request where your dog can engage with you.
  • Making every interaction a test. Include ordinary companionship without a stream of requests. Save practice for moments you can arrange thoughtfully.
  • Giving everyone a different visitor rule. Agree on the barrier, reward, and endpoint before the guest arrives.
  • Waiting for grabbing before preparing a toy. Put your equipment in place before the predictable trigger, while you can still move calmly.
  • Adding duration, distance, and movement together. Change one feature so you can identify what made the task harder.
  • Expecting a birthday to finish the work. Judge readiness by the demonstrated behavior in that setting.

Know when the home plan is insufficient

If anyone feels unable to manage the interaction, stop rehearsing it and arrange individual support from a qualified reward-based behavior professional. Share your event log and describe exactly what happens. Do not ask children or worried visitors to participate in a training experiment.

FAQ: adolescent Great Dane behavior

Will my Great Dane become calm at exactly two years old?

Do not use a birthday as a deadline. Keep the routines that help your dog succeed, and expand freedom according to demonstrated behavior in each setting.

Should I repeat a cue when my Dane ignores it?

Pause and make the next attempt easier. Try a quieter setting or more distance from the distraction, then reward the response you wanted.

What should I prepare before an evening nipping episode?

Have an appropriate toy within adult reach and a comfortable way to create space. Rehearse the replacement behavior before the usual trigger appears.

Should I chase my dog during indoor zoomies?

Keep people out of the running path and avoid turning the event into a chase. Clear obstacles and plan the environment before the next predictable burst.

Can I practice greetings without letting visitors touch my dog?

Yes. A useful rehearsal can end with your dog quietly observing a seated visitor from behind a comfortable barrier. A physical greeting is optional.

How do I tell whether the plan is helping?

Compare similar situations in your event log. Look for easier redirection, fewer interrupted rehearsals, or a familiar behavior working with a little more distraction.

A workable way through adolescence

The Great Dane teenage phase calls for clear routines you can repeat on an ordinary day: prevent the predictable scramble, practice a replacement in a manageable setting, and reward the response you want. Start with the next leash pickup or visitor arrival, keep a brief record, and let demonstrated skills guide the next step.
